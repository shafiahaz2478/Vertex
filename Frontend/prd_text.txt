Distributed Road Hazard Intelligence & Public Accountability System1. Project OverviewWorking ConceptA software platform that uses vehicle dashcam footage as a distributed sensing network to detect and map road hazards—primarily potholes—and make road-condition information publicly visible.Instead of depending only on government road surveys, dedicated road-monitoring vehicles, or sensors installed on major highways, the system collects road-condition data from ordinary vehicles travelling across different types of roads.The platform processes dashcam footage, identifies road hazards, determines their geographic location, assigns a severity/confidence score, and displays them on an interactive map.The public can therefore see:Where potholes and other road hazards existHow severe they areWhich roads are affectedWhich routes are relatively safer/better maintainedHow recently a hazard was detectedWhether authorities have acknowledged or resolved the issueA government/authority dashboard can subsequently use the same data to:Identify high-priority roadsTrack unresolved hazardsMonitor recurring problem locationsAssign maintenance prioritiesRecord action takenDemonstrate improvement over time2. Core ProblemRoad hazards such as potholes create:Vehicle damageTwo-wheeler accidentsTraffic congestionReduced road safetyIncreased travel timePoor emergency responsePublic dissatisfactionThe major problem is not only that potholes exist.The deeper problem is:There is no sufficiently continuous, distributed and transparent system for discovering, locating, prioritising and publicly tracking road hazards across all types of roads.Traditional road monitoring can be:Periodic rather than continuousLimited by manpowerFocused on major roadsExpensive to scaleSlow to reflect newly formed hazardsDifficult for citizens to independently verify3. Core InsightVehicles can become distributed road sensors.A vehicle already travels through roads every day.A dashcam already observes the road.Therefore, instead of deploying expensive dedicated infrastructure everywhere:Vehicle → Dashcam → Road footage → AI detection → Location → Hazard database → Public mapThis creates a continuously growing road-condition dataset.The system does not require every vehicle to participate.Even a relatively small number of participating vehicles can gradually build a road-condition map.4. Primary ObjectiveThe primary objective is:To create a scalable platform that continuously collects road-condition observations from vehicles, detects and geolocates road hazards, and converts them into publicly accessible and actionable road-safety intelligence.The MVP should prove this complete chain:Capture → Detect → Locate → Store → VisualiseEverything else should come after this.5. Hazard ModelInitially, focus on potholes.Do NOT attempt to detect every possible road problem.Future hazard classes could include:PotholesCracksRoad debrisBroken road surfacesWaterloggingMissing manhole coversDamaged signsFallen objectsRoad obstructionsPoor lane markingsDamaged speed breakersUnlit hazardsBut the first version should be:Pothole detection done well.10. Severity ClassificationA pothole should not simply be:Pothole = YesThe system should attempt to estimate severity.Example:LowSmall surface defect.MediumPotentially dangerous for two-wheelers.HighLarge/deep pothole capable of causing significant vehicle instability or damage.For MVP, severity can initially be based on:Estimated sizeVisual characteristicsDetection confidenceRepeated observationsLater it can incorporate:Vehicle speedCamera perspectiveDepth estimationSuspension/IMU dataCrowd confirmation11. Confidence vs SeverityThese must be kept separate.ConfidenceHow confident the AI is that the object is a pothole.Example:Pothole probability = 94%SeverityHow dangerous the pothole appears.Example:Severity = HighA system could therefore produce:Confidence: 96%Severity: Mediumor:Confidence: 81%Severity: HighThis distinction makes the system considerably more credible.12. Multiple Vehicle ObservationsOne of the most important future capabilities is crowdsourced verification.Suppose:Vehicle A → detects potholeVehicle B → detects same potholeVehicle C → detects same potholeThe backend should recognise that these are probably the same physical hazard.Instead of creating three potholes:Pothole #145Observed by:3 vehiclesConfidence:Very HighFirst detected:10 AugLast detected:22 AugStatus:UnresolvedThis makes the dataset much more reliable.13. Duplicate DetectionThis should eventually become a backend capability.If two detections occur within a geographic radius:Detection ALat: XLon: YDetection BLat: X + small differenceLon: Y + small differencethe system can determine that they refer to the same hazard.Possible future logic:Spatial distance+Time difference+Visual similarity=Same hazard probabilityFor MVP, a simple geographic threshold is sufficient.14. AI / Computer Vision PipelineThe initial AI pipeline should be simple.Video ↓Frame extraction ↓Pothole detection model ↓Bounding box ↓Confidence ↓Frame timestamp ↓GPS association ↓Hazard recordPotential technologies can include:YOLO-based object detectionOpenCVPythonPyTorchONNXCloud inference or local inferenceThe exact model should be chosen based on accuracy, inference speed and availability of training data, not because it sounds impressive.22. Geolocation ChallengeOne of the hardest technical problems is:How do we know exactly where the pothole in the video is?A dashcam frame itself does not necessarily contain GPS information.Possible solutions:Option 1 — GPS-equipped dashcamBest real-world approach.Option 2 — Smartphone GPSPhone provides location while camera captures footage.Option 3 — GPX/video timestamp synchronizationSynchronize:Video timestamp+GPS timestampOption 4 — Demo simulationFor hackathon:Use a recorded route with associated GPS points and demonstrate the complete pipeline.Do not pretend simulated GPS is real-time production infrastructure.23. Privacy and SecurityThis must be considered from the beginning.Dashcam footage may contain:FacesVehicle registration platesPedestriansPrivate locationsThe platform should therefore avoid exposing raw footage publicly.Possible privacy pipeline:Raw video ↓AI processing ↓Blur faces / number plates ↓Store only required evidence ↓Public mapFor the MVP, the public application should primarily expose:Hazard locationHazard typeSeverityDetection dateStatusrather than entire raw videos.24. The Core Product LoopThe entire platform can ultimately be reduced to this loop:DRIVE  ↓CAPTURE  ↓DETECT  ↓LOCATE  ↓VERIFY  ↓MAP  ↓PRIORITIZE  ↓ACT  ↓VERIFY AGAIN  ↓RESOLVEThe final verify again step is particularly powerful.If another vehicle later drives over the same road and the pothole is no longer detected:Before:High severityAfter:No hazard detectedthe system has evidence that the road condition changed.That turns the platform from a reporting system into a road-condition monitoring system.29. Long-Term VisionThe long-term system is not simply a pothole map.It can become:A real-time digital layer representing the physical condition of a city's roads.Every participating vehicle contributes observations.Over time, the platform can understand:Which roads deteriorate fastest?Which areas have recurring problems?Which hazards remain unresolved longest?Which roads are improving?Where should maintenance happen first?Eventually:Current Road Condition          +Historical Data          +Traffic          +Weather          +Maintenance History          ↓Predictive Road IntelligenceThis could support proactive maintenance rather than waiting for accidents or complaints.30. What the Team Should Ask Before Every FeatureWhenever someone proposes a feature, ask:Question 1Does this directly improve detection, location, understanding, visibility or resolution of road hazards?If no → probably postpone it.Question 2Does this help us prove the core product loop?If no → P1/P2.Question 3Are we building a real capability or just making the demo look impressive?This is especially important during a hackathon.Question 4Can this feature be removed without breaking the core system?If yes → it is probably not core.36.
